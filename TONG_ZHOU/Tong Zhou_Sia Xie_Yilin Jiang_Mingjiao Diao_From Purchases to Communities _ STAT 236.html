A SIMULATION STUDY OF PRODUCT NETWORKS

From Purchases
to Communities

Buying more can reveal a community.
It can also obscure one.

We study how user activity and preference breadth shape product networks, and when reducing the influence of active buyers helps community recovery.

Short answer: down-weighting active buyers hurts when they are focused. It helps when they buy broadly and the other users carry enough signal.

01 / THE QUESTION

Should frequent buyers
count less?

A buyer purchasing d distinct products contributes d(d − 1)/2 product pairs. A few active buyers can dominate a projected product network.

But their influence may be useful. If they favor a category, their purchases help reveal it. If they buy broadly with no category preference, they add connections without expected category contrast.

Our question is whether activity weighting suppresses unwanted background or removes useful evidence.

02 / THE EXPERIMENT

Same purchases.
Different buyers.

Each simulated dataset has 1,000 users, 300 products, and 3 equal product categories with known labels. Because the categories are known, we can score every method exactly. In the main design we change who makes the purchases while holding the total number of purchases fixed, so method differences are not driven by the amount of data.

Preferences. Each user has a preferred category: a product in that category is r times as likely to be chosen as any other product. We study three preference strengths, r=2, r=3, and r=4. Under all users focused, every user keeps this preference. Under active users broad, the most active 20% buy uniformly at random (r=1), while everyone else stays focused.

Activity. Purchase counts follow one of three distributions with exactly matched means. Low heterogeneity gives everyone the same count; medium and high heterogeneity move purchases to the most active 20% of users. Counts are assigned independently of preferred category.

How one dataset is generated

  1. Products. Split the 300 products into 3 categories of 100. These are the true labels we try to recover.
  2. Preferred category. Each user draws a preferred category uniformly at random.
  3. Purchase count. Each user draws a purchase count d from the activity distribution in the table below, independently of the preferred category.
  4. Preference strength. Focused users get strength r. Under active users broad, the most active 20% get r=1 instead.
  5. Purchases. The user buys d distinct products one at a time, without replacement. Each remaining product in the preferred category has weight r; every other product has weight 1.

Users are generated independently of each other. A single user's purchases are not independent, because the same product cannot be bought twice. The first purchase lands in the preferred category with probability r/(r+2): about 1/2, 3/5, and 2/3 for r=2, 3, and 4. Focused active users resemble heavy buyers loyal to one category; broad active users resemble resellers or corporate accounts that buy across the catalogue. Full details, including a worked example, are in Appendix A of the report.

03 / FOUR METHODS

One dataset.
Four representations.

Every dataset is analyzed four ways. Three methods build a product network and cluster it with the same spectral procedure; the fourth works on the user–product matrix directly. Misclustering rate is the fraction of products assigned to the wrong category after label matching.

01 Count Shared purchasers

Every shared buyer adds 1 to a product pair. We normalize the product network, extract its leading spectral embedding, and cluster the products.

W(i,j) = Σ B(u,i) B(u,j)
02 Jaccard Overlap relative to union

Shared buyers are divided by buyers of either product. This adjusts overlap for product purchasing frequency, then uses the same spectral procedure.

W(i,j) = shared / (buyers(i) + buyers(j) − shared)
03 Activity weighted Less influence per active buyer

A user who buys d products contributes 1/(d−1) to each pair. Their total pair weight grows linearly instead of quadratically. Users buying fewer than two products contribute no pairs.

W(i,j) = Σ B(u,i) B(u,j) / (d(u) − 1)
04 Normalized bipartite SVD Direct matrix representation

We normalize the user-product matrix by user and product degrees, extract its leading three right singular vectors, normalize embedding rows, and cluster products. This is a baseline inspired by bipartite spectral co-clustering, not a reproduction of a published algorithm. Because MTM weights each user by 1/d(u), it is itself an activity-weighted projection.

M = Du^(−1/2) B Dp^(−1/2)

Every method uses the true K=3 and k-means with 20 starts. Product projection diagonals are removed; the SVD representation retains the implicit effect of its Gram diagonal. Seeds and software versions are recorded.

05 / THE KEY FINDING

Activity is not a measure
of information quality.

Focused active buyers carry the signal. At r=2, with focused preferences and high heterogeneity at mean 12, weighting raises error from 13.04% to 48.60%. The paired difference favors Count by 35.57 percentage points, with a 95% interval of 34.14–36.99 points. Downweighting these buyers discards the strongest evidence in the data.

Broad active buyers add noise. With broad active users at medium heterogeneity and mean 12, weighting helps only modestly at r=2: 62.60% to 60.23%, both close to the error of random labels (about 63%). Stronger preferences change this: weighting reaches 34.77% at r=3 and 9.55% at r=4, while Count stays near 61–63%. At high heterogeneity, every method stays near the random-label level at every r, because the remaining informative users buy only one or two products each.

Jaccard tracks Count, and SVD tracks Weighted. Jaccard adjusts for product popularity, which is equal in our design, so it has nothing to correct. The bipartite SVD weights each user by 1/d(u), so it behaves like activity weighting; at mean 6 it also suffers from the 800 single-purchase users, and removing them brings it in line with Count (report, Section 4.5).

One open question. In Design 1, turning active users broad also takes purchases away from informative users, so poor recovery could mean too much noise or too little signal. Design 2 below separates the two.

06 / DESIGN 2 · FIXED INFORMATION

Keep the information.
Then add background.

Why a second design. Design 1 keeps total purchases fixed. When the most active 20% become broad buyers, two things happen at once: heavy buyers with no category preference are added, and the purchases they absorb are taken away from informative users. When every method ends near random labels, we cannot tell whether weighting failed to suppress the noise or whether there was too little signal left to recover. We added Design 2 after the r=2 results showed this confound.

What it does. We fix 1,000 informative users in one of two settings (12 purchases each with r=2, or 6 purchases each with r=3) and leave their purchases untouched. We then add 50 or 200 users with no category preference who each buy 1, 2, 4, or 8 times as many products as an informative user (D/m), plus one baseline with no added users. This gives 18 conditions with 100 datasets each. Within a replicate, the informative purchases are identical across conditions, so any change in error comes from the added background. Total purchases grow as background is added, unlike Design 1.

What to look for. Weighting can only help if activity marks the noisy users. When background users buy as much as informative users (D/m=1), weighting gives them the same weight, and Count and Weighted return identical partitions. With 200 background users, Weighted also loses a few points: it limits each noisy user to linear influence, but many such users still add up.

Fixed information, added background. With m=12 and r=2, 50 background users buying 48 products each raise Count error to 59.05%, while Weighted gives 36.02%. Without background, both give 35.53%.

All methods in this section share a k-means seed within each dataset, which makes the exact equivalence of Count and Weighted at D/m=1 visible. The random-label reference here is computed per condition by permuting the true labels 20 times per dataset; it lies between 62.9% and 63.5%, which is the dashed line used in the results explorer above.

07 / SCOPE AND LIMITS

A controlled comparison.

The experiments use synthetic data, equal category sizes, equal product weights within categories, and a known number of communities. We do not claim the same ranking for real purchase data or every SVD method.

Count and Jaccard have very similar average recovery here but do not always return the same partition. At low activity heterogeneity, Count and Weighted have the same normalized network in exact arithmetic.

Projected edges are dependent, so we do not apply detection thresholds derived for independent-edge network models. Confidence intervals describe Monte Carlo precision under this simulation model, and multiple comparisons are unadjusted. The sparse, high-heterogeneity broad-active setting has no expected category contrast in the pair projections, so no projection can recover it.

In practice the choice between Count and weighting depends on whether heavy buyers are informative, which is not known in advance. The activity distribution is observable: when it is nearly even the two methods agree, and when a few users dominate, comparing the two partitions, or checking whether heavy buyers concentrate in one cluster, indicates which regime applies.
