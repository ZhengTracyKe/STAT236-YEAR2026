Compression Causes Non-Monotonic AI-Text Detection

Eric Tong, Katie Murphy and Klara Barbić · Harvard University

OpAI-Bench edits a human document in eight rounds, each touching more sentences. You would expect detectors to find the text easier to flag every round. Instead they get worse at v4. At v4 three things change at once: half the sentences are now AI-edited, the edit is a compression, and the text gets shorter. We show the dip comes from the compress operation, not from the amount of AI text or the length.

Research questions

Four questions, four answers

We use the public OpAI-Bench release (essays, news, government reports and scientific abstracts, edited by four LLMs) and 431 news articles from 2016, before LLM writing tools, that we edited ourselves on the same schedule. Our main detector is a Higher Criticism word classifier built from course methods.

  • Q1. Is the v4 dip real?With a transparent detector built from course methods, and on new data? Yes. It appears on the benchmark and on 431 pre-LLM news articles we edited ourselves.
  • Q2. Operation or amount?Is the dip caused by compressing, or by reaching 50% AI-edited text? The operation. Holding coverage fixed, compression is always hardest to detect.
  • Q3. Is it just length?Compressed text is shorter. Is that why it is harder? No. Length-matched comparisons leave the gap unchanged.
  • Q4. What does compression change?Which features of the text explain the gap? Paraphrase and expand add AI-associated words. Compression mostly removes words.

Methodology

How a Higher Criticism word classifier finds AI edits

An LLM edit nudges the frequency of many words by a little. Most words carry no signal; a few carry a weak one. This "rare and weak" setting is where Higher Criticism (Donoho & Jin, 2004, 2008) works well. We trained the classifier on 10,976 OpAI-Bench training documents, each human original paired with one randomly chosen edited version.

Step 1

Turn each document into word frequencies

Lowercase the text, split it into words, and count. Dividing by document length gives a frequency fj = countj / n, so long and short documents are comparable. We keep the 13,284 words that appear in at least 20 training documents.

Step 3

Test every word, then let Higher Criticism pick the cut

For each word a Welch two-sample test compares human and AI-edited documents and gives a z-score and p-value. Under no signal the z-scores would follow the dashed bell curve; the real ones have long tails. Sorting the 13,284 p-values, Higher Criticism measures how far the smallest ones beat chance:

HC(m) = √P · (m/P − p(m)) / √(m/P · (1 − m/P))

We keep the words whose p-value is at or below p(m*), where m* maximizes HC(m) over the smallest α₀·P p-values. Cross-validation chose α₀ = 0.05, so the search stops at 664 words (|z| ≥ 6.08). HC is still rising there; without the cap it would peak near 2,700 words.

Step 4

Give each selected word a color

Each selected word is AI-leaning (z < 0) or human-leaning (z > 0); the darker the color, the stronger the evidence. The model weights each word by the sign of z divided by its standard deviation, so a rare word that shifts strongly, like nevertheless, moves the score far more than the.

Step 5

Score an unknown document

Color the document's words, then add up their pushes. Each use of word j moves the score by wj/n, and a fixed baseline centers the score at the training average:

L(d) = Σj wj (fj − μj)

Low scores look AI-edited. The document is flagged when L(d) < τ = −13.2, a threshold chosen by cross-validation (below). Load a random article from the test split, switch between its human original and its AI-edited version, or type your own text.

Choosing the parameters

How we tuned the classifier: cross-validation

Four choices shape the classifier: α₀, how far HC searches; min_df, how many training documents a word must appear in; frequencies or raw counts; and whether each word is divided by its standard deviation. We tried all 48 combinations with 5-fold cross-validation on the training split, keeping all versions of a document in the same fold.

The five settings with the highest average AUROC were compared once on the dev split, and the one with the best dev accuracy won: α₀ = 0.05, min_df = 20, standardized frequencies. It also has a theoretical case: frequencies remove document length, and standardizing lets rare but telling words count. The threshold τ = −13.2 is the accuracy-maximizing cut, averaged over the five folds.

Discussion

Takeaways

How a text is edited matters

Detectability depends on the operation, not only on how much of the text is AI-edited or how long it is. OpAI-Bench's "mixed-authorship region" at v4 is better described as a compression effect.

Compression is a blind spot

Compressing removes obvious LLM vocabulary and adds little of its own. Detectors that lean on word choice, and the zero-shot Fast-DetectGPT in the benchmark paper, all lose ground at v4.

Limits

Ablation 2 is one generator (Gemini 2.5 Flash) and 120 test documents. Our own edits use one model and one domain, and the pretrained detectors read only the first 512 tokens. The word decomposition is algebraic, not a causal mediation analysis.

Results · Q1

The v4 dip reproduces

On the OpAI-Bench test split our HC classifier reaches accuracy 0.738 and AUROC 0.796. Like the sixteen detectors in the benchmark paper, its accuracy rises through v3, falls at the compression step, and recovers at v5. The dip also shows up in every domain, for models trained only on v0 vs. v4, for two TF-IDF classifiers on the benchmark and on our Newsroom articles, and for the pretrained E5-Small detector.

Results · Q2

It is the operation, not the amount of AI text

Ablation 2 holds coverage fixed and varies the operation. Compression is the hardest edit to detect at every coverage, and among compressed documents there is no dip at 50%, only a slow rise. On our own data we kept the whole trajectory and swapped only the v4 compress for a paraphrase: the dip disappears.

Results · Q3

Is compressed text harder to catch just because it is shorter?

Compression is the only step in the schedule that makes documents shorter. A shorter document gives a word-frequency detector fewer words to count, so its evidence is noisier, and that alone could lower detection at v4. We tested this three ways. None of them makes the gap go away.

v4 is the only version that gets shorter

The median document falls from 374 words at v3 to 333 at v4. Expansion at v5 then makes documents longer than the originals, and they stay longer through v8.

The dip appears at every length

We split the test documents into five groups by the length of their human original (fifths within each domain), so a document and all its versions stay in one group. The dip at v4 shows up in every group, from the shortest fifth to the longest.

Test 1: compare documents of the same length

For each compressed v4 document we found documents from other sources with the same domain, the same generator and a length within ±5% at v3 (or v5, or v2). The gap barely moves after matching. At equal length the detector still catches about 25 points fewer v4 documents than v3 documents, and a v4 document is flagged about as often as a same-length v2 document, which has half as much AI-edited text.

Test 2: cut the longer version down to the same length

Ablation 2 gives each document a compressed version and a paraphrased or expanded version of the same sentences. We cut the longer one to exactly the compressed version's token count, by keeping a prefix, a random sample of its tokens, or a random window, and scored it again with the same model and threshold. The advantage over compression stays the same size.

If shortness alone made text look AI-edited, cutting human originals would raise false positives. We removed the same number of tokens from each of the 120 human originals in ablation 2 (56 tokens on average), 20 times over, and scored them with the same threshold.

Which words make the difference

Because the score is a sum over words, the equal-length score gap between paraphrased and compressed documents splits exactly into word contributions. AI-leaning words carry 49.6 of the 53.9-point gap (92%). Paraphrase brings in nevertheless, numerous, straightforward; compression does not. Only a few function words push the other way: compression deletes of and the, which on their own make compressed text look slightly more AI-like.
