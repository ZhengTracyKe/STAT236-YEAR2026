When people edit AI text sentence by sentence, the watermark survives in contiguous chunks. Tests that ignore token order cannot use this, but summing evidence within chunks and running Higher Criticism across chunks can.

Abstract

A Gumbel-max watermark is detected from one “pivot” per token, which is uniform on text the watermarked model did not produce and pushed toward 111 on text it did. After human editing, only a small fraction of tokens keep the watermark. The existing optimal theory treats these survivors as scattered independently, and its tests ignore token order. In practice, when a person rewrites some sentences and keeps others, or pastes AI passages into their own writing, the survivors instead form contiguous segments. We formalize this by letting the alternative be that the watermark survives in segments of at least ℓ\ellℓ tokens, and we study the resulting problem within an explicit theoretical sandbox.

We show that tests which ignore token order remain stuck at the old detection boundary however long the segments are, and that contiguity helps exactly when one segment carries enough evidence, as measured by its expected log-likelihood ratio. At the critical scale, Higher Criticism applied across blocks of tokens attains a sharp boundary, which reaches full detectability exactly when one segment's expected log-likelihood ratio exceeds log⁡N\log NlogN, the log of the number of blocks. A key-only count test captures most of the gain, and simulations agree with the theory.

From Watermark to Hypothesis Test

This section walks through how a watermark is planted during generation, what the detector actually observes, and how editing turns detection into the testing problem we study.

2.Pivots and the null hypothesis

The detector sees the text and holds the key, but not the model. For each token it regenerates the numbers from the key and the preceding hhh tokens and keeps the one belonging to the word that actually appears, the pivot Yt=Ut,wtY_t=U_{t,w_t}Yt​=Ut,wt​​. If a person wrote the token, they chose it without looking at the key, so YtY_tYt​ is exactly uniform on [0,1][0,1][0,1]. This gives a null hypothesis that needs no model of human writing. If the watermarked model wrote the token, the word was chosen because its number was large relative to its probability, so YtY_tYt​ is pushed toward 111. Its law, worked out by Li et al. (2025), depends only on PtP_tPt​. Detection is therefore a hypothesis test on Y1,…,YnY_1,\ldots,Y_nY1​,…,Yn​, with all pivots uniform under the null.

3.How much evidence a token carries

How far a pivot is pushed toward 111 depends on how much freedom the model had. If the model was nearly certain of the next word, the watermark had nothing to choose and the pivot is close to uniform. We measure this freedom by Δt=1−max⁡wPt,w\Delta_t=1-\max_wP_{t,w}Δt​=1−maxw​Pt,w​, the probability that the model does not pick its top word, and we call such a non-greedy choice an event. Events are where the evidence comes from. A word of probability Δ\DeltaΔ can win the argmax only if its number was within about Δ\DeltaΔ of 111, so an event leaves a pivot extremely close to 111, while a greedy choice leaves almost no trace.

The cleanest case, and the hardest one to detect among models with freedom Δ\DeltaΔ (Li et al., 2025), is the two-point law. Here the top word has probability 1−Δ1-\Delta1−Δ and a single alternative has probability Δ\DeltaΔ, so the watermarked pivot is nearly uniform plus a spike of mass about Δ\DeltaΔ just below 111. A detector without the model cannot see events directly, but it can count pivots near 111. Under the null a fraction uΔu\DeltauΔ of pivots land above 1−uΔ1-u\Delta1−uΔ, and events roughly double this rate. A segment of ℓ\ellℓ watermarked tokens contains about ℓΔ\ell\DeltaℓΔ events. More generally, a token's expected evidence is its expected log-likelihood ratio, written KL\mathrm{KL}KL, which is about 0.21 Δ0.21\,\Delta0.21Δ when Δ\DeltaΔ is small.

4.Editing, and why the watermark survives in runs

Because the numbers at step ttt are seeded by the previous hhh tokens, a token keeps its watermark only if it and its hhh predecessors are unchanged. Change any of them and the detector regenerates unrelated numbers, so the pivot looks uniform even if the token itself was untouched. After editing, only a fraction ε\varepsilonε of tokens keep the watermark, which is the rare and weak setting where Higher Criticism (HC) is the natural detector. Li, Ruan, Wang, Long and Su (2025) worked out the optimal theory assuming each token survives independently. Real edits rarely look like that. A person who keeps some AI sentences and rewrites others leaves the watermark intact on whole sentences, and a pasted AI passage survives as one run. The number, positions and lengths of the runs are unspecified, so a test must work for every arrangement. The hardest arrangement turns out to be one in which each sentence-sized block survives independently, which is Li et al.'s model one level up, with blocks in place of tokens. Because the sparsity is now over blocks, the model suggests a detector that sums the evidence within each block and then applies HC across blocks.

5.The sandbox

All of our results are proved within an explicit idealized model, and we state its assumptions openly so that it is clear what the theorems do and do not cover.

  • Pivot model (from Li et al.). Given which tokens survive, the pivots are independent, uniform on non-surviving tokens and drawn from the watermark law on surviving ones. This ignores repeated contexts and the effect of edits on the model's probabilities.
  • Segment survival (new). The alternative is the run model of step 4, with no model of the editor.
  • Bounded freedom (from Li et al.). Surviving tokens have freedom at least Δ\DeltaΔ. Our sharpest results instead fix the next-token distributions and give them to an oracle detector, and we also study a key-only detector that never uses them.
  • Asymptotic calibration (as in class). The parameters scale as powers of nnn, as described next.

As in the Rare/Weak model from class, we let each parameter scale as a power of the text length nnn and ask, as n→∞n\to\inftyn→∞, for which exponents detection is possible. The answer is a region in the plane of exponents, called a phase diagram, with a sharp boundary between the part where some test succeeds and the part where every test fails. Main Results

Our results build on one another. The first is negative and shows that the usual detectors cannot benefit from contiguity at all. The second identifies when a segment-aware test can benefit, and the third pins down exactly how much it gains. The fourth shows that most of this gain is available without knowing the model.

1.Order-blind tests are stuck

Any test that depends only on the histogram of pivots, such as the sum test, token-level HC or Li et al.'s Tr-GoF, has exactly the old detection boundary however long the segments are. Shuffling the tokens does not change what such a test sees, so it cannot tell segments from scattered survivors.

2.Contiguity means evidence per segment

A token carries evidence only when the model had some freedom of choice. Contiguity therefore helps exactly when a whole segment accumulates enough evidence, as measured by its expected log-likelihood ratio, which is roughly the number of events it contains.

3.A sharp boundary for HC across blocks

Between these two regimes lies a critical scale, where HC across blocks attains the optimal boundary. This boundary always improves on the order-blind one and reaches full detectability exactly when one segment's evidence exceeds log⁡N\log NlogN, where NNN is the number of blocks.

4.A key-only test

The optimal test needs the model's probabilities, which a detector rarely has. Simply counting near-1 pivots in each block and running HC across blocks needs only the secret key and still recovers most of the gain.

Simulations

The theory is asymptotic, so we also check it in simulation. We generate pivots directly, drawing them uniformly on unwatermarked tokens and from the watermark law on the surviving set. The surviving set is either scattered at random (ℓ=1\ell=1ℓ=1) or made of runs of length ℓ\ellℓ, and power is the fraction of watermarked texts detected at false-positive rate 0.05.

On the order-blind side we run the sum test, which is the standard Gumbel-max detector, and token HC, which applies HC over all tokens. The segment-aware tests, labeled psi* in the figures, split the text into blocks of length ℓ\ellℓ, compute a p-value for each block and run HC across blocks together with a Bonferroni check. Count psi* counts near-1 pivots in each block and needs only the key, whereas oracle LLR psi* uses the exact block likelihood ratio and so needs the model's probabilities. Finally, the combined test rejects if either the sum test or count psi* does. In these runs the block tests are given the true segment length and block boundaries, which is a best case for them, and the paper reports a check without this knowledge.

Takeaways and Limitations

The practical message is that when edits happen at the level of sentences or passages, a detector should look at where the large pivots occur and not only at how many there are. Since the block test and the classic sum test each win in different regimes, running both and rejecting if either does is a sensible default. How much contiguity helps depends on the text. Low-entropy text such as code or short factual answers carries little evidence per token, so there the gain appears only for long runs.

These conclusions come with caveats. All of our results hold within an explicit sandbox, which assumes independent pivots, an idealized segment model and large nnn, and all of our experiments are synthetic. Most simulations also use long texts of 50,000 tokens or more, although the essay-length check above shows the same mechanism at 2,000 tokens. The natural next step is to test the theory on real data, using GPT-2 with our own watermark key on news text, statistics abstracts and code. This would measure how much evidence real sentences carry and allow copy-paste and sentence-retention demonstrations.
