Did school board meetings become partisan?

When, and in what sense?

We analyze 80,063 school board meeting transcripts from 1,485 districts to trace education politics surrounding COVID. The frequency of contested topics, such as critical race theory, book content and parental rights, rose sharply during the pandemic, and so did the hostility of speakers’ comments (scored by a language model), in Republican- and Democratic-leaning counties alike. Where the two diverged was in the positions speakers argued, not the topics they mentioned: in late 2021, speakers in Republican-leaning counties advocated conservative positions more than speakers in Democratic-leaning counties, mostly in public comment (the part of a meeting where parents and residents speak to the board). By 2024, speakers in Republican-leaning counties advocated conservative positions about as rarely as those in Democratic-leaning counties, and in both, nearly as rarely as before the pandemic.

01

What a meeting looks like as data

We leverage DistrictView, a newly available dataset of U.S. school board meeting transcripts from YouTube. Of 122,987 videos, we drop those with no transcript, fewer than 1,000 words or a missing file, then screen out non-board meetings using titles, descriptions and opening text. The final dataset has 80,063 meetings from 1,485 districts in 49 states: about 1.13 billion words and 140,000 hours of video. The median meeting has about 12,100 words and lasts 1.5 hours.

The transcripts are automatic captions: no reliable punctuation, transcription errors and almost no speaker information, so we cannot directly separate board members from public commenters. Public comment is the agenda item where parents, residents and students address the board, usually for a few minutes each; section 04 uses an LLM to estimate how much of each excerpt is public comment. There is no vote count for a school district, so we measure a district’s partisanship by the county it sits in: the Republican share of the county’s two-party (Republican plus Democratic) presidential vote in 2020. Districts in counties above 0.5 are “Republican-county” districts and the rest “Democratic-county” districts; 0.61, for example, means 61% of the two-party vote went Republican. This is only a proxy: a county can contain several districts with different politics, and some districts cross county lines.

02

Analysis methods

Our question: how much conflict is there in school board meetings, which topics do boards argue about, and how does this differ between Democratic- and Republican-leaning counties before, during and after COVID? To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale analysis of rhetoric in local education politics surrounding COVID. We pair supervised methods, which measure agendas named in advance, with unsupervised ones that let topics emerge from the data.

Higher Criticism
Tests 11,804 words at once for any change in word share between 2018–19 and 2022–23 in 273 matched districts, so many small shifts add up without picking words in advance. Six phrase dictionaries follow up on contested topics.
Vocabulary clearly changed: HC+ = 859.85 against a 95th-percentile bootstrap threshold of 11.34 (819.30 without COVID-related, number-format and suspected artifact words). Equity/inclusion, race/racism, book-restriction and parents’-rights mentions rose; school choice and closures did not change.

Topic models (LDA) & spectral clustering
LDA describes each ~1,000-word chunk as a mix of topics. We fit K = 10, 20 and 30 and use K = 20: it has the highest coherence while remaining interpretable. K = 30 has slightly lower perplexity, but topic diversity keeps falling and it splits themes into near-duplicate subtopics. Spectral clustering then groups districts into three clusters by their topic weights.
Routine governance dominates. COVID/health and parental-concerns topics peaked in 2020–21 and returned to pre-COVID levels by 2023; motions and voting kept rising. The three clusters lean Democratic, Republican and neutral, and track partisanship beyond locale, with a mild polarization during COVID.
Dominated by procedural vocabulary; a topic is a distribution over words, not a position.

03

Contested-topic mentions rose in both groups

Mentions of contested topics such as COVID mandates, critical race theory, book content and parental rights rose in both Republican- and Democratic-leaning districts, and followed the same course in both: COVID mandates and CRT peaked in 2021 and waned, while parental rights, book content and gender stayed elevated into 2023. In 2022–23, mentions of CRT, book content, parental rights and school choice did not differ detectably between the two groups. The largest differences were in other themes: equity and social-emotional learning (more in Democratic-leaning counties) and athletics and facilities (more in Republican-leaning counties).

04

The positions split, briefly

One important limitation of word-count-based methods is that they do not reveal which side a speaker takes. We therefore use a large language model to answer focused positional questions about 28,374 excerpts of about 1,500 words from 348 districts, each scored 0–100 on six attributes. Before the pandemic, speakers rarely argued conservative positions. In 2021, speakers in both groups argued them more, and speakers in Republican-leaning counties argued them more than those in Democratic-leaning counties.

How the scoring works

We give an off-the-shelf language model (not trained or fine-tuned on our data) a passage of a meeting and a short list of questions; it returns one number from 0 to 100 per question. The questions we write are the whole measure.

Step 1Cut and anonymizeFrom each meeting we take a random ~1,500-word window, plus up to two centered on contested-topic words. Names become [PLACE] and [NAME], so the model can’t infer a district’s politics from them.
Step 2Ask with a rubricGABRIEL sends the excerpt and a short definition of each attribute to gpt-6-luna in one prompt. The wording was tested on 200 pilot excerpts, revised once, then frozen.
Step 3Read back numbersThe model returns a 0–100 score for each of the six attributes. A separate pass estimates how much of the excerpt is public comment.

What it measures

Share of the excerpt that is public comment by members of the public speaking to the board, as opposed to board member or staff discussion. Cues: “state your name and address”, “three minutes”, “next speaker”, “public comment”, a speaker introducing themselves as a parent or resident.

Most meeting time is routine business that scores 0, so the 2018–19 averages were only 0.3–0.4 in both groups. 05

One county demonstration

To make the averages concrete, we follow one district through the period and quote what was said, in order.

Why Indian River County, Florida

Indian River is one of the three Republican-leaning districts in our sample where speakers’ conservative-positions scores rose most from 2018–19 to 2021–24 (+27.5 points on the 0–100 scale, the largest in Florida). It is also a center of Moms for Liberty, a parental-rights movement founded in 2021 that the Southern Poverty Law Center classifies as far-right. It is an extreme case, chosen to illustrate the scores, not a typical district.

What a 0.61 Republican share means

In 2020, 61% of the county’s two-party presidential vote went to the Republican candidate. Anything above 0.50 counts as Republican-leaning; at 0.61, Indian River is clearly Republican-leaning but not extreme, about one “10-point step” above an even split in the event study’s units.

Each entry is one sentence from a meeting, with the conservative-positions score of its ~1,500-word excerpt. For comparison, the average random excerpt in Republican-county districts scored 6.8 in 2021 and 0.3–0.4 in 2018–19. These come from the district’s highest-scoring excerpts, so they show what high scores look like, not typical meeting content. Of the 27 verified Indian River quotes with a public-comment estimate, 20 are mostly public comment. The topics trace the national pattern: masks in 2020–21, then library books from 2022 onward. The percentage is the model’s estimate of how much of the ~1,500-word excerpt is members of the public speaking rather than board members or staff: 48% means about half of it is public comment, 100% all of it, and 0% (August 2021) none of it, meaning board or staff discussion. It describes the whole excerpt, not just the quoted sentence.
