Does influential feature selection help identify market regimes?

We cluster 479 weeks of financial and macroeconomic data into calm and stressed regimes, and test whether IF-PCA's feature screening beats clustering on all features.

Cindy Sun · Xiaoman Xu · Eric Xue · Cecilia Zheng
479weeks, Aug 2017 – Oct 2026
42features after filtering
5clustering methods compared
96holdout weeks, models frozen

Short answer: no. With 42 features and 383 training weeks, screening removes useful signal. Among the methods that find two substantial regimes, plain K-Means on all features gives the best-separated, most stable, and most persistent split. Which features go in matters at least as much as the clustering method.

The setup

Why this question

Markets alternate between calm and stressed periods, and correlations and risk premia change with them. We treat each week as a point in 42-dimensional space and look for regimes as clusters of weeks.

IF-PCA (Jin and Wang, 2016) was built for data with far more features than observations. It keeps only features whose distribution departs from a normal, as measured by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) statistic, then clusters on the leading singular vectors. We ask whether that screening still helps when there are fewer features than weeks.

Data. A snapshot of daily FMP and FRED data, aligned to Friday-ending weeks: ETF returns and realized volatility, Treasury yields and spreads, oil and the dollar, the VIX, and macro indicators such as unemployment and industrial production.

No look-ahead. Macro data enter only after their release lag (7 weeks for monthly, 18 for quarterly series). Feature filtering uses only the first five years. The last 20% of weeks are a holdout scored with frozen models.

Result 2

KS screening keeps outliers and drops volatility

  • The top six KS scores all belong to macro series (unemployment, claims, GDP, industrial production, fed funds, retail sales). Most of them sit near zero except in a few extreme weeks, so their non-normality comes largely from outliers, not regimes.
  • No volatility feature ranks in the top 15, and only 2 of the 12 make IF-PCA 50%'s top 21. So IF-PCA 50% splits on equity and oil returns, which flip sign often: 62 regime switches in 383 weeks.
  • The Higher Criticism threshold keeps 7 features, but its peak (0.363) is reached in 40% of simulations with no signal at all. The evidence for a sparse set of influential features is weak.
Result 3

The regime is a volatility regime, and it needs many volatility features

Agreement (ARI) with the baseline K-Means partition when we change the feature set. 1 means the same split; 0 means unrelated.

  • K-Means' two regimes differ most in the ETF realized volatilities and the VIX (1.3–1.8 standard deviations apart).
  • Removing only near-duplicates barely changes the split (ARI 0.91). Removing the ETF volatilities, or squeezing all 12 into one component, breaks it (ARI 0.23–0.25), and the regimes switch 62–64 times.
  • The correlation-aware filter recovers most of IF-PCA's gap for the same reason. It drops redundant return features and lets in one volatility feature, HYG realized volatility (KS rank 26). Remove that one feature and agreement falls from 0.80 to 0.44.

Takeaways

What we learned

Screening is for p ≫ n

IF-PCA removes noise when many useless features swamp the signal. With 42 features and 383 weeks, it mostly removes signal: keeping 25%, 50%, or 75% of features all score below K-Means.

Non-normal ≠ informative

Heavy tails, step-shaped monthly series, and serial dependence all raise the KS statistic. The screen rewards outliers, so it ranks the volatility features that define the regimes low.

Inputs shape the regimes

Change the feature set and the same algorithm tracks volatility, returns, or short rates. Filtering correlated features is a cheap fix that brings IF-PCA close to K-Means with half the features.

Limitations. Adjacent weeks are dependent, so the effective sample is smaller than 479, and stability rests on two window comparisons. Separation metrics are computed in the full feature space, which favors K-Means. Regimes have no ground truth: historical events help us interpret them but cannot validate them.
